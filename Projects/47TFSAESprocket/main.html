47T FSAE Sprocket

A design study for a 47-tooth FSAE sprocket focused on reducing weight while preserving the requirements needed for reliable drivetrain service.

Overview

Goal

Reduce the sprocket weight by at least 10% while maintaining the functional, packaging, and manufacturing requirements of the FSAE drivetrain.

Design requirements

The sprocket must reduce weight by at least 10% while meeting the drivetrain interface requirements. It must use the existing 47-tooth layout, fit an ISO 606 428 motorcycle chain, and mate with the Drexler V3 160 mm differential used by the car.

Current status

The sprocket and hub have been CAD modeled and evaluated with hand calculations and an ANSYS static structural simulation. A drawing has been submitted to the manufacturer for a quote, while the sprocket hub is planned for in-house machining on a 3-axis Haas CNC mill. The design work is organized around the five-step process below.

Optimization Theory

From motor torque to tooth loading

The motor produces a maximum torque of 107 N·m. The 10-tooth motor sprocket has a 41 mm pitch-circle diameter, so the motor torque is converted into chain pull using:

\[ M = Fr \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad F = \frac{M}{r} \]

The chain pull is then combined with the chain's centrifugal tension. The resulting total chain tension is approximately 5,357 N. Because the force is not shared evenly, the first engaged tooth carries the greatest load and the force decreases according to the back-tension calculation.

The back-tension values show that the first 11 teeth carry approximately 99% of the total load. This localized load path is the key reason the hub can be optimized instead of treating the entire circular hub as equally loaded.

Shear-stress model

Topology optimization begins at the 35 mm radius, because the hub must remain 27 mm thick from the center out to the 70 mm diameter needed to fit the differential. For a radius \(r\) beyond that region, the loaded arc is approximated as \(11/47\) of the circumference:

\[ A = Lt = \left(\frac{11}{47}\right)2\pi r t \]

Using the acting moment and the force at any radius gives the shear stress:

\[ F(r) = \frac{M_{acting}}{r} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \tau = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{M_{acting}}{\left(\frac{11}{47}\right)2\pi r^2 t} \]

This equation shows why material is most valuable near the smaller radii: stress decreases with (r^2). Material can therefore be removed farther from the center while retaining load-carrying material around the hub and bolt connections.

Safety factor and topology decision

The hand calculations set a target design factor of safety of at least 3. Using the maximum-shear-stress assumption, shear strength is approximated as half of tensile yield strength. For 7075-T6 aluminum, the calculation uses a tensile yield strength of approximately 503 MPa and solves for the required thickness:

\[ n_d = \frac{S_{ys}}{\tau} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad t = \frac{6M_{acting}}{\left(\frac{11}{47}\right)2\pi r^2 S_{yt}} \]

The resulting minimum solid-hub thickness is approximately 6 mm. Because the hub needs cutouts for heat dissipation and to limit flexing across the thin cross-section, the design increases the local thickness to 7.31 mm, matching the thickness used successfully in last year's geometry.

With thickness known, the required material at each radius is converted into an angular span:

\[ L = \left(\frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\right)2\pi r \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \theta = 360^\circ\left(\frac{6M_{acting}}{2\pi r^2 S_{yt}t}\right) \]

Those angles were plotted at different radii to guide the hub topology. The material was then patterned around the four mounting locations so the design remained lightweight while maintaining a continuous load path.

Material and bolt decisions

Weight reduction comes from replacing the previous bulky steel construction with more aluminum while retaining steel where the drivetrain interfaces require it. 7075-T6 aluminum has a density of approximately 2.81 g/cm³, compared with approximately 7.85 g/cm³ for 4340 steel. The M10 mounting bolts were selected after iterating the bearing-yield calculation; the documented configuration produced a factor of safety of 4.245 and required approximately 9.23 mm of local thickness at the bore.

\[ \sigma_{bearing} = \frac{F}{Dt} \]

The spline shear analysis was not repeated because the spline is supplied by Drexler. The hub was instead evaluated around the shear-stress, bolt-bearing, and mounting-interface load paths.

Process

Step One / Determine requirements

The first step was defining the interfaces and performance target for the sprocket:

  • Reduce sprocket weight by at least 10%.
  • Use the required 47-tooth geometry.
  • Fit an ISO 606 428 motorcycle chain.
  • Fit the Drexler V3 160 mm differential hub.
  • Maintain four mounting bores, matching last year's assembly baseline.

Interface constraints

The ISO 606 chain standard controls the tooth profile and pitch. I modeled the chain rollers and chain-plate clearance, then added approximately 4 mm of clearance to determine the maximum hub diameter. A 15 mm gap between that maximum hub diameter and the bolt bores set the bore radius at 70 mm, or 140 mm bore-circle diameter.

Step Two / Determine loading and restraints

The primary load case starts with 107 N·m of motor torque. The 10-tooth motor sprocket's 41 mm pitch-circle diameter produces approximately 5,357 N of total chain tension after the chain-pull and centrifugal-tension contributions are combined. The back-tension calculation distributes this force across the engaged teeth, with the first 11 teeth carrying approximately 99% of the total load.

Worst-case load path

The worst-case assumption is differential lockup: the sprocket and hub resist the motor-applied tooth loads while the spline is fixed. The reactions are transferred through the sprocket web, four mounting bolts, and hub into the Drexler differential interface.

Step Three / Hand calculations

Hand calculations were used to convert motor torque into chain tension and tooth loading before running finite-element analysis. The hub checks covered shear stress at each radius and bearing yield at the mounting bores. The sprocket checks covered bearing yield at the bolt locations and tooth shear; spline shear was not repeated because the spline is supplied by Drexler.

Calculation basis

The calculation workbook documents the tooth-force calculation, the 99%-of-load first-11-teeth assumption, the thickness sizing, and the bolt-bearing iteration. The M10 bolt configuration produced a documented bearing factor of safety of 4.245 with approximately 9.23 mm of local thickness.

Step Four / CAD the sprocket and hub

The sprocket and hub were modeled around the 428 chain interface and Drexler differential mounting requirements. The hub retained 27 mm of thickness in the differential-fit region, then used the calculated 7.31 mm minimum local thickness as the starting point for the lightweight web and cutouts.

CAD focus

The CAD stage connected the tooth profile, chain clearance, four-bolt mounting pattern, spline interface, sprocket web, and differential hub into one assembly-ready design. The cutouts reduce mass while preserving the load path and improving the hub's heat-dissipation potential.

47T Manufacturing Drawing

The drawing shows the 47-tooth profile, 428 chain geometry, four-bolt pattern, hub dimensions, and the selected 7.31 mm section thickness.

Step Five / ANSYS static simulation

The final step was setting up an ANSYS static structural simulation using the calculated tooth loading and hub restraints. The bolt preload was set to 75% of bolt strength, the spline was fixed to represent the differential interface, and the sprocket teeth were loaded using the back-tension values.

Validation

The design was assessed by comparing the finite-element response with the analytical load path and the original weight-reduction target. The simulation report is available in the project resources below.

Design and FEA Comparison

Old versus revised sprocket

The revised sprocket changes the web geometry and integrates the hub interface for the Drexler differential. The side-by-side FEA results show how each design responds to its documented static structural setup. The minimum factor of safety is below 1.0 in both results and is concentrated at the mounting bores, but the revised design shows higher factors of safety throughout much of the rest of the part. The previous year's sprocket operated without failure during vehicle use, and the revised design has a similar overall factor-of-safety response, supporting use of the simulated geometry as a design basis while the bore regions remain areas for attention.
